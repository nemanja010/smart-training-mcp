# Periodization for Runners

## Core concept

Periodization is the systematic variation of training volume, intensity, and focus over time to maximize performance at a target event and prevent over-training.

**Key principle:** You cannot do everything at once. Trying to build volume, intensity, and speed simultaneously leads to injury, stagnation, or burnout.

---

## Annual / macrocycle structure

```
General Prep → Specific Prep → Pre-competition → Competition → Transition (recovery)
```

For a runner targeting one or two goal races per year:

```
Base (8–16 wk) → Build (8–12 wk) → Peak (3–4 wk) → Taper (2–3 wk) → Race → Recovery (2–4 wk)
```

---

## Phase breakdown

### Base phase

**Duration:** 8–16 weeks
**CTL target:** +3–5 TSS/week
**Volume:** Progressive — increase 10% every 3 weeks, then back off 1 recovery week
**Intensity:** 90–95% Z1/Z2, max 5–10% Z3 (strides and occasional threshold)

**Goals:**
- Build aerobic base (mitochondrial density, capillary density, fat oxidation)
- Strengthen tendons, ligaments, and bones (they adapt slower than cardiovascular system)
- Establish weekly mileage that forms the platform for build-phase quality work
- No pressure — runs should feel easy

**Key sessions:**
- Long run (weekly, building length)
- Easy daily miles
- Strides 2–3 × per week after easy runs
- Optional: hill repeats (strength without track intensity)

**What to avoid:** Structured intervals, tempo work, VO2max sessions. Not because they don't work, but because the mileage base isn't ready to absorb the load without injury.

---

### Build phase

**Duration:** 8–12 weeks
**CTL target:** +3–8 TSS/week
**Volume:** Maintain or slight increase (no longer the priority)
**Intensity:** 80/20 split (polarized) or Daniels quality sessions 2× per week

**Goals:**
- Raise VO2max and lactate threshold on top of the aerobic base
- Develop race-specific energy systems
- Accumulate quality work without breaking down

**Key sessions (choose by goal distance):**

| Distance | Priority 1 | Priority 2 | Long run focus |
|---------|-----------|-----------|----------------|
| 5K | VO2max intervals (I pace) | Repetitions (R pace) | Easy long, strides |
| 10K | VO2max + threshold | Tempo or cruise intervals | Easy long |
| Half marathon | Threshold / cruise intervals | VO2max intervals | Half marathon-pace segments |
| Marathon | Long runs with M-pace sections | Threshold | Marathon-pace long runs |

**Microcycle (weekly rhythm):**
```
Hard / Easy / Hard / Easy / Easy / Easy / Long
```

Never two hard sessions in a row. Long run is always followed by rest or easy.

---

### Peak phase

**Duration:** 3–4 weeks
**CTL:** Maintain; small volume reduction begins
**Intensity:** Race-specific; reduce volume of quality work, but maintain sharpness

**Goals:**
- Race-specific neuromuscular preparation
- Maintain all fitness gains from build
- Begin psychological and physiological sharpening

**Key sessions:**
- Race-pace work (reps at goal race pace)
- Tune-up race (70–80% effort, not all-out; or 2–3 weeks before goal race)
- Shorter, sharper intervals replace longer threshold blocks
- Long run slightly shorter

**Tune-up race strategy:**
- 5K tune-up → useful 3–4 weeks before a 10K
- 10K tune-up → useful 3–4 weeks before a half marathon
- Half marathon tune-up → useful 4–5 weeks before a marathon
- Don't race within 2 weeks of goal race for long events

---

### Taper phase

**Duration:** 2–3 weeks for half marathon; 3 weeks for marathon
**CTL:** Allow TSB to rise to +5 to +25 range
**Volume:** Cut 40–60% from peak week
**Intensity:** Maintain! Cut volume, not intensity.

**Goals:**
- Replenish glycogen stores
- Repair micro-damage from training
- Nervous system freshness
- Rise TSB to optimal race window (+5 to +25)

**Taper madness:** Runners commonly feel sluggish, heavy, or fear losing fitness during taper. This is normal and physiologically expected. Fitness is maintained with reduced volume; the body is consolidating adaptations.

**Weekly taper schedule (marathon, 3 weeks):**
```
Week 3 out: 80% of peak volume, maintain 1 quality session
Week 2 out: 60% of peak volume, 1 shorter quality session
Race week: 40% of peak volume, strides, easy runs, race
```

---

### Recovery phase (post-race)

**Duration:** 1 week per race hour as a rough guide (2:00 marathon = 2 weeks; ultra = longer)

**Timeline:**
- Days 1–3: Complete rest or very easy walking
- Days 4–7: Short easy runs (20–30 min) if legs feel OK
- Week 2: Easy running, no structure
- Week 3–4: Return to normal easy volume
- Week 5–6: Can begin introducing light quality work

**Why recovery matters:** The immune system, muscle tissue, and connective tissue need genuine recovery. Training too soon after a marathon increases injury risk substantially.

---

## Mesocycle structure (4-week blocks)

Within each phase, use 3-week progressive + 1-week recovery pattern:

```
Week 1: Base volume
Week 2: +10% volume or +1 quality session
Week 3: +10% volume (peak of this block)
Week 4: Recovery — 60–70% of week 3 volume, easy only
```

Then start next block at week 3 volume level (don't go back to week 1).

**Why the recovery week matters:** Most adaptation happens during recovery, not during hard work. Skipping recovery weeks consistently leads to overtraining syndrome.

---

## CTL progression guidelines

| Athlete level | Safe weekly CTL increase | Notes |
|--------------|--------------------------|-------|
| Beginner (<2 yr) | 2–4 TSS/week | Tendons/bones haven't adapted yet |
| Recreational (2–5 yr) | 3–6 TSS/week | Can absorb more if healthy |
| Experienced (5+ yr) | 5–8 TSS/week | High base means more stress needed |
| Elite | 6–10 TSS/week | Base CTL 80–150; high capacity |

**Warning signs of too-fast CTL increase:**
- ACWR > 1.4
- TSB < −35
- HRV trending down 3+ days
- Persistent muscle soreness (not DOMS — ongoing)
- Performance declining despite training

## Block periodization (advanced)

An alternative to traditional linear periodization. Concentrates specific stimuli in focused 3–4 week blocks rather than mixing everything throughout.

**Accumulation block (3–4 wk):** High volume, low intensity. Build aerobic base.
**Transmutation block (3–4 wk):** Moderate volume, high intensity. Race-specific stimuli.
**Realization block (1–2 wk):** Low volume, race-pace sharpening. Taper.

**Advantages:** Deeper adaptation per quality because the body isn't being pulled in multiple directions simultaneously.
**Disadvantages:** Requires precise planning; each block depends on the previous one going well.

## Training year for a runner with two goal races

### Single-peak year (one major race)
```
Jan–Mar: Base (16 weeks)
Apr–Jun: Build (10 weeks)
Jul:     Peak (3 weeks)
Aug 1:   Goal race
Aug:     Recovery (3–4 weeks)
Sep–Oct: Maintenance / secondary racing
Nov–Dec: Transition / strength / off-season
```

### Two-peak year (spring + fall goal races)
```
Jan–Feb:  Build toward spring race
Mar:      Taper
Apr:      Goal race 1
Apr:      Recovery (2–3 weeks)
May–Jul:  Base rebuild + build
Aug:      Peak
Sep:      Taper
Oct:      Goal race 2
Nov–Dec:  Recovery + off-season
```

**Key:** The second peak requires less base-building if the spring race was well-executed, since aerobic fitness was maintained.

## Special considerations

### Building mileage for the first time

**Never increase total weekly volume by more than 10%.**
Use the 10% rule as a ceiling, not a target. Slower is safer.

**Add a fifth day before making long runs longer.** Frequency adaptations are less stressful than single-session length.

### Coming back from injury

- Don't try to make up for lost time
- Restart at 50–60% of pre-injury volume
- First 2 weeks: all easy, no quality
- Week 3: reintroduce first quality session (short)
- Week 4 onward: rebuild at ≤10% per week

### Year-round strength training integration

- Off-season / Base: 2–3 sessions/week (higher volume, lower intensity)
- Build: 1–2 sessions/week (maintain)
- Peak / Taper: 1 session/week (maintenance only, no new stimulus)

See `references/injuries.md` for strength exercises specific to injury prevention.
