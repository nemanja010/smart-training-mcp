# Training Methodologies

## Polarized Training (80/20)

### Source
Stephen Seiler (Norwegian School of Sport Sciences). Research on elite endurance athletes across multiple sports (rowing, cross-country skiing, cycling, running).

### Core principle
Elite endurance athletes do ~80% of training at low intensity (Z1 — below VT1/first ventilatory threshold) and ~20% at high intensity (Z3 — above VT2/second ventilatory threshold). They spend very little time in the "medium intensity" zone (Z2 — between VT1 and VT2).

### Why it works
- Low-intensity work maximizes volume without accumulating excessive fatigue
- High-intensity work drives VO2max and neuromuscular adaptations
- The "gray zone" (Z2 — moderate effort) is metabolically taxing without providing proportionally more adaptation signal
- More total high-quality time at Z3 is achievable because Z1 runs don't compromise recovery

### Practical application

**Weekly structure:**
- 4–6 runs per week
- 3–5 easy/long runs at Z1 (below conversational threshold)
- 1–2 hard sessions at Z3 (threshold, VO2max, or race pace)
- Aim for zero sessions in Z2 (no "moderate" effort runs)

**How to execute Z1 correctly:**
- Use heart rate: stay below 82% of LTHR
- Use talk test: full conversation at all times
- Use Stryd: stay below 75% CP
- On hills: reduce pace to maintain HR, not pace

**What to cut when reducing volume:** Cut Z1 volume first. Keep the Z3 sessions.

**Evidence:** Seiler (2010) analyzed elite rowers — 75% Z1, 5% Z2, 20% Z3. Similar distribution found in elite runners, cyclists, and triathletes.

## Jack Daniels VDOT System

### Source
Jack Daniels, *Daniels' Running Formula* (1st ed. 1998, 3rd ed. 2014).

### Core principle
Each runner has a VDOT — an aerobic profile number derived from race performance that predicts appropriate training paces for all zones (E, M, T, I, R). Workouts are precisely paced using these zones.

### VDOT
- Not the same as measured VO2max (which requires lab equipment)
- Derived from race time + Daniels' formula: accounts for VO2max but also running economy
- Two runners with the same lab VO2max may have different VDOTs if one has better economy
- Update VDOT after each goal race or time trial

### The five paces

| Pace | Anchor | Purpose | Volume per week |
|------|--------|---------|-----------------|
| E (Easy) | 59–74% VO2max | Volume accumulation, recovery | 60–80% of all running |
| M (Marathon) | 75–84% VO2max | Aerobic development | 0–29 km/week |
| T (Threshold) | 83–88% VO2max | LT improvement | ≤10% of weekly mileage |
| I (Interval) | 97–100% VO2max | VO2max improvement | ≤8% of weekly mileage |
| R (Repetition) | >100% VO2max | Economy, speed | ≤5% of weekly mileage |

### Quality session volume caps
- I pace: no more than 8% of weekly mileage per session
- R pace: no more than 5% of weekly mileage per session
- T pace: no more than 10% of weekly mileage, but sustained tempo maximum 20–40 min

### Annual structure (Daniels phases)
1. **Phase I (Foundation):** E runs + strides. Build base, no intensity.
2. **Phase II (Early quality):** Add R and I work. Speed before LT.
3. **Phase III (Transition quality):** Shift to T and M work. LT and marathon focus.
4. **Phase IV (Race-specific):** Race-pace work, tune-up races.

## Pfitzinger (Pete Pfitz)

### Source
Pete Pfitzinger & Scott Douglas, *Advanced Marathoning* (1st ed. 1999, 3rd ed. 2019). Also *Faster Road Racing* for 5K–half marathon.

### Core principle
High weekly mileage base is the foundation. Quality work layered on top. No shortcuts to volume.

### Typical marathon plans
- 12-week, 18-week plans at 55, 70, or 85+ miles/week
- Most runs are genuine easy pace
- 2 quality sessions per week (medium-long run + long run + 1 workout)

### Key Pfitzinger concepts

**Medium-long run:** 13–19 km. Bridges the gap between easy daily runs and the long run. Underused by most runners but crucial for marathon development.

**Long run specifics:**
- Up to 35 km (22 miles) for marathon prep
- Some at marathon pace or with late-race surges
- Done on tired legs (not after a rest day)

**Lactate threshold workouts:** 5–9 km of continuous threshold running. Emphasis on marathon pace when marathon is the goal.

**Recovery:** Pfitzinger builds in easy days very seriously. "You don't get fitter from hard workouts — you get fitter from recovering from hard workouts."

**Injury prevention:** Mileage ramp of ≤10% per week. No consecutive hard days.

## Norwegian Double Threshold Method

### Source
Developed by Renato Canova and refined by Norwegian coaches (Gjert Ingebrigtsen, working with Jakob, Henrik, Filip Ingebrigtsen). Popularized internationally ~2020–2022.

### Core principle
Perform two threshold sessions in a single day (morning and afternoon), keeping both strictly at LT1–LT2 (ideally verified with lactate testing at ~2 mmol/L). Easy volume on other days.

### Why it differs from normal threshold work
- Traditional approach: 1 threshold session/week, rest of week easy
- Norwegian approach: 2 threshold sessions on 2–3 days/week = 4–6 threshold sessions per week
- Key: keeping sessions truly submaximal (lactate ~2 mmol/L, not the traditional 4 mmol/L "threshold") allows recovery within the same day

### Weekly structure (elite application)
```
Mon: AM easy 10 km + PM easy 8 km
Tue: AM threshold 10 × 1 km @ LT + PM threshold 5 × 2 km @ LT
Wed: Easy 14 km
Thu: AM threshold 4 × 4 km @ LT + PM threshold 8 × 1 km @ LT
Fri: Easy 12 km
Sat: AM easy 18 km long run
Sun: Rest or easy 8 km
```

### Lactate control
The original protocol uses finger-prick lactate monitoring after each rep. Target: 1.8–2.5 mmol/L. If lactate rises above 2.5, reduce pace.

### Practical adaptation without lactate testing
- Use pace at which you can complete reps with limited perceived exertion (RPE 6–7/10)
- Verify with HR: should be 85–92% of LTHR (slightly sub-threshold)
- The pace is slower than traditional "tempo" — it should feel almost easy for the first few reps

### Prerequisites
- CTL > 70, several years of consistent running
- Strong aerobic base
- Good recovery capacity
- Not recommended for beginners or runners building initial base

## Lydiard Method

### Source
Arthur Lydiard (New Zealand), developed in 1950s–60s. Coached Peter Snell and Murray Halberg to Olympic gold medals.

### Core principle
Long periodized development in four phases. Maximum aerobic base first, then sharpening. Running form and easy mileage before any intensity.

### The four phases

**1. Aerobic base (3–5 months)**
- High volume, all easy (conversational pace)
- "100 miles a week" (Lydiard's prescription; proportionally scale to individual)
- No speedwork, no intervals
- Goal: build maximum aerobic capacity and fat-burning efficiency

**2. Anaerobic development (4 weeks)**
- Hill running: uphill for strength, downhill for form, bounding drills
- Short sprint-type efforts (steep hills)
- Transitions the body from purely aerobic work to faster-twitch activation

**3. Speed / sharpening (4 weeks)**
- Track work: intervals at race pace and faster
- Short, sharp, full-recovery
- Goal: express the aerobic base as race speed

**4. Racing / coordination (4 weeks)**
- Tune-up races every 2 weeks
- Maintenance runs
- Prepare the body for peak competition

### Modern application
Lydiard's four-phase model underpins most modern periodization frameworks. The key insight — build the aerobic engine before adding quality — is foundational. Modern coaches compress the timeline but maintain the principle.

## Hanson Method

### Source
Luke and Kevin Hanson, *Hansons Marathon Method* (2012).

### Core principle
Moderate high volume (50–60 mpw) with an emphasis on "cumulative fatigue." The long run is capped at 26 km (16 miles) — the longest run is never the longest it should be in isolation; it is always run on cumulative fatigue.

### Key concepts

**Cumulative fatigue:** Every run is run on tired legs. This simulates the physiological state of miles 18–26 of a marathon, even during a 16-mile long run.

**Long run cap:** 16 miles (26 km). Counterintuitive, but Hansons argue that with 6 days of running per week, the long run represents only 25–30% of weekly mileage (vs. the traditional 30–40%). The fatigue at the end of a 16-miler on tired legs matches the end-of-race fatigue of a 20-miler on fresh legs.

**Workout days (Tuesday/Thursday):**
- Varied: speed work, strength intervals, tempo runs
- Prescribed at marathon pace + 10 sec/mile, marathon pace, or slightly faster

**SOS (Something of Substance) sessions:**
- Long run (Sunday)
- Two quality sessions (Tuesday, Thursday)
- Everything else is "easy" support

**Not for everyone:** Requires 5–6 days of running per week. The approach can work very well for runners who have previously "bonked" late in marathons by under-preparing legs for cumulative fatigue.

## Comparison: which method for which runner

| Runner profile | Best method | Reason |
|---------------|-------------|--------|
| Beginner (<3 years running) | Daniels or Lydiard base-first | Builds proper foundation |
| Injury-prone | Polarized (80/20) | Reduces Z2 gray-zone stress |
| 5K–10K focus | Daniels VDOT | Systematic pace-based quality |
| Half marathon | Daniels or Pfitzinger | LT and long run emphasis |
| Marathon, intermediate | Hanson or Pfitzinger 55 mpw | Proven marathon programs |
| Marathon, advanced | Pfitzinger 70–85 mpw | Maximum volume + quality |
| Elite / very experienced | Norwegian double threshold | Highest LT volume possible |
| Building multi-year base | Lydiard | Long-term aerobic foundation |

## Seiler's 5 key sessions (practical polarized template)

These are the five session types that Seiler's research identifies as constituting most elite endurance training:

1. **Long easy run** (75–90 min at Z1) — once weekly
2. **Double easy runs** (2 × 45 min Z1, same day) — 2–3 times weekly
3. **VO2max intervals** (4–8 × 4 min at Z3, equal rest) — once weekly
4. **Threshold intervals** (3–5 × 8–10 min at T pace, 2 min rest) — once weekly
5. **Race-pace speed** (short, fast, specific to goal) — once weekly in peak phase

This produces the 80/20 split automatically without complex planning.
