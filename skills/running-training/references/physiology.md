# Running Physiology

## The three pillars of running performance

Running performance is determined by three interdependent physiological factors:

```
Running performance = VO2max × Lactate threshold (% VO2max) × Running economy
```

Improving any one of them improves performance. The most trainable in well-trained runners is running economy and lactate threshold.

---

## VO2max (maximal aerobic capacity)

**Definition:** Maximum rate of oxygen consumption per unit time and body mass (ml/kg/min).

**Typical values:**

| Population | VO2max (ml/kg/min) |
|-----------|-------------------|
| Untrained adult male | 35–45 |
| Recreational runner, male | 45–55 |
| Serious amateur, male | 55–65 |
| Elite male runner | 70–85 |
| World-class male (Kipchoge estimate) | ~89 |
| Untrained adult female | 30–40 |
| Serious amateur, female | 50–60 |
| Elite female runner | 65–75 |

**What limits VO2max:** Primarily cardiac output (stroke volume × heart rate). Peripheral factors (oxygen extraction, capillary density) are secondary but still trainable.

**How to train VO2max:** VO2max intervals (4–8 min at I pace, equal rest). Must spend time at or near 95–100% VO2max for adaptation. Short intervals (Billat 30/30) can also drive this.

**Trainability:** VO2max responds well in early training (20–30% increase possible) but approaches a genetic ceiling. Beyond ~3 years of serious training, VO2max improvements are small. Threshold and economy improvements continue much longer.

**Vascular adaptation timeline:** 6–8 weeks for measurable cardiac improvements; 12–16 weeks for full adaptation to a new stimulus.

## Lactate threshold (LT1 and LT2)

**LT1 (Aerobic threshold, VT1):** The exercise intensity at which lactate begins to rise above baseline. Corresponds to ~2 mmol/L. This is the top of "easy" Z1 training.

**LT2 (Anaerobic threshold, VT2, OBLA):** The exercise intensity at which lactate accumulates faster than it can be cleared. Corresponds to ~4 mmol/L. This is threshold/tempo pace.

**Why LT2 matters more for performance:**
- Elite marathoners can sustain 90–95% of VO2max pace at LT2
- Recreational runners: ~75–80% of VO2max
- Improving LT2 means you can run faster before lactate cascades — directly improves marathon and half marathon pace

**How to train LT2:** Sustained tempo (20–40 min), cruise intervals, marathon-pace long runs. The Norwegian method maximizes time just below LT2.

**Relationship between VO2max and LT:**
- A runner with VO2max 60 and LT2 at 85% VO2max will outperform a runner with VO2max 65 and LT2 at 75% VO2max in a marathon.
- LT2 as a % of VO2max is highly trainable; this is where most improvement comes from in experienced runners.

---

## Running economy (RE)

**Definition:** Oxygen cost of running at a given submaximal pace. Lower oxygen cost = better economy.

**Why it matters:** Two runners with identical VO2max can differ by 20%+ in marathon performance if one has significantly better RE. Kipchoge's RE is extraordinary.

**Factors affecting running economy:**
- Stride length and cadence (optimal varies per runner)
- Ground contact time (shorter = more economical)
- Vertical oscillation (lower = better)
- Leg spring stiffness (higher = more elastic energy return)
- Running form (forward lean, arm swing, hip drive)
- Shoe stack/carbon plate (Nike Vaporfly provides ~4% RE improvement)
- Altitude training (increases RBC count and oxygen-carrying capacity)
- Neuromuscular adaptations from speed and hill work

**How to train RE:**
- Strides and R-pace repetitions (improve neuromuscular power)
- Hill repeats (improves strength and form)
- Plyometrics and strength training (increases leg spring stiffness)
- High mileage base (chronic adaptation over years)
- Downhill running at controlled effort

**Timeline:** RE improvements are gradual and accumulate over months–years of consistent training.

## Heart rate physiology

**Maximum heart rate (HRmax):**
- Genetically determined; declines ~1 bpm/year after ~30
- Formula estimates (220 − age) are unreliable (SD ≈ 10–12 bpm)
- Best measure: sprint to exhaustion after full warmup, or note peak HR in all-out race
- Not trainable — high HRmax doesn't predict performance

**Resting heart rate (RHR):**
- Lower in aerobically fit athletes (30–40 bpm in elite; 60–80 typical adult)
- Increases with illness, stress, over-training, dehydration, heat
- Measure upon waking before getting out of bed
- RHR >5 bpm above baseline: check HRV, consider easy day

**Cardiac drift (aerobic decoupling):**
- HR gradually rises during a sustained steady-state run even when pace is constant
- Caused by fluid shifts, muscle fatigue, thermoregulation demand
- Quantified as % HR increase per % of run elapsed
- Aerobic decoupling >5% in easy zone = poor aerobic fitness, too hot, dehydrated, or running too fast
- As fitness improves, decoupling decreases
- Intervals.icu calculates this automatically as "Aerobic Decoupling" in activity analysis

**HR reserve (HRR):** HRmax − HRresting. Used in Karvonen formula for zone calculation (more accurate than % HRmax alone).

## Heart rate variability (HRV)

**What it is:** Beat-to-beat variation in heart rate. Measured as RMSSD (ms). Higher RMSSD = better parasympathetic (recovery) state.

**Why it matters for training:**
- Reflects autonomic nervous system (ANS) balance
- Low HRV = sympathetic dominance (stress, fatigue, illness)
- High HRV = parasympathetic dominance (recovered, adapted)
- More sensitive than RHR to training stress
- Best measured first thing in the morning before activity

**Practical interpretation:**
- Don't react to a single day — look at 7-day rolling average (baseline)
- >15% below baseline: significantly suppressed — consider easy or rest
- 8–15% below baseline: moderately suppressed — reduce intensity, not duration
- Within 5% of baseline: normal variation — proceed with planned session
- Trending down 3+ consecutive days: overreaching signal regardless of absolute value

**App tools:** HRV4Training (research-backed), Elite HRV, Garmin Body Battery, Whoop recovery score.
**Metrics:** RMSSD is most validated for daily monitoring. ln(RMSSD) is more normally distributed and used in HRV4Training.

## VO2max vs. training age and development phases

| Training age | Primary adaptation to target |
|-------------|------------------------------|
| 0–2 years | VO2max and basic aerobic base |
| 2–5 years | Lactate threshold and mileage |
| 5–10 years | Running economy and specificity |
| 10+ years | Fine-tuning, race tactics, maintainance |

This is why beginners improve rapidly and experienced runners need more stimulus variation.

---

## Adaptation timeline

| Adaptation | Timeline |
|-----------|----------|
| Stroke volume increase | 6–8 weeks |
| Mitochondrial density | 4–6 weeks |
| Capillary density | 8–12 weeks |
| Tendon/ligament strengthening | 12–24 weeks |
| Bone stress adaptation | 16–26 weeks |
| VO2max peak adaptation | 8–12 weeks at new stimulus |
| LT improvement | 4–8 weeks |
| Running economy | Months to years |

**Implication:** Tendons and bones adapt much slower than the cardiovascular system. This is why rapid mileage increases cause stress fractures and tendinopathy even when the heart and lungs feel fine.

## Altitude and heat physiology

### Altitude training
- Live High / Train Low (LHTH) is the gold standard for elite runners
- 2200–2500m altitude: adequate hypoxic stimulus
- 3–4 weeks minimum for hematological adaptations (↑ EPO → ↑ RBC)
- Performance benefit: 1–3% at sea level for 2–3 weeks post-altitude
- Training at altitude: easy paces are faster due to reduced air resistance, but VO2max is reduced — train by HR/RPE, not pace

### Heat adaptation
- 10–14 days of heat training (running in hot conditions) increases plasma volume (~8–10%)
- This is beneficial even for cool-weather racing — more blood volume = higher cardiac output
- Start with easy runs in heat; allow 5 days for initial acclimatization

### Dehydration effects on running
- 2% body mass dehydration: performance drops 2–10%
- 3–5%: ↑ HR +3–8 bpm at same pace, ↑ perceived effort
- >5%: heat illness risk; performance severely impaired
- Running in heat: drink to thirst; avoid overdrinking (hyponatremia risk in long events)

## Sleep and performance

**Why sleep matters:**
- Growth hormone (GH) is secreted during slow-wave sleep — critical for muscle repair and adaptation
- Cognitive performance (race tactics, pacing judgment) declines sharply with <7 hrs sleep
- HRV reflects cumulative sleep debt
- REM sleep consolidates motor learning (form improvements persist with good sleep)

**Research findings:**
- Sleep extension (from 6.5 → 9.5 hr) in athletes: ↑ sprint speed, ↑ reaction time, ↓ fatigue
- One night of 4–5 hr sleep: next-day performance drops ~10%, subjective feel drops more
- Cumulative sleep debt: doesn't fully recover with a single night of extra sleep

**Practical targets:**
- 8–9 hours in hard training phases
- Sleep quality matters (HRV monitors this indirectly)
- Naps (20 min) before afternoon quality sessions are beneficial

## Fueling physiology (brief)

**Fat vs. carbohydrate oxidation:**
- At Z1 effort: 50–70% fat, 30–50% CHO
- At Z3 threshold: ~80% CHO, ~20% fat
- At VO2max: nearly 100% CHO
- Fat stores: ~80,000+ kcal — essentially unlimited
- Glycogen stores: 400–500g (liver + muscle) = 1600–2000 kcal = ~90–120 min at threshold

**Hitting the wall in marathons:**
- Glycogen depletion at ~20 miles (32 km) in an undertrained or under-fueled runner
- Training at easy pace trains fat oxidation — shifts crossover point to higher intensity
- Carbohydrate intake during marathon: 60–90g/hr for optimal performance (multiple transportable CHO: glucose + fructose)
