# Plyometrics Training Skill Spec for Running Performance

## Overview

This skill defines principles, constraints, and protocols for using plyometric training to improve running performance, running economy, and overall fitness for endurance and recreational runners.
It is designed to be consumed by AI coding agents (e.g., OpenCode, ClaudeCode) that generate training plans, analyze workouts, or give coaching feedback.
[^1][^2][^3]

The skill focuses on short, high-quality sessions (10ΓÇô30 minutes of plyo exposure) that can be integrated into busy schedules and combined with running, cycling, or strength training plans.
It emphasizes progression, injury risk management, and evidence-based dosing drawn from peer-reviewed studies and coaching practice.
[^4][^2][^5][^6]

## Definitions and Concepts

### What Plyometrics Are

- Plyometric training = explosive movements that use the stretchΓÇôshortening cycle (SSC), typically involving rapid eccentric loading followed by a brief amortization phase and powerful concentric action (e.g., hops, bounds, jumps).
[^2][^7][^8]
- Primary adaptations: increased lower-body power and stiffness, improved use of elastic energy, better neuromuscular coordination, and improved landing mechanics.
[^5][^8][^2]
- For runners, the key outcome is improved running economy (RE) ΓÇö less oxygen at a given speed ΓÇö rather than large changes in VOΓéémax.
[^9][^10][^6]

### Why Plyometrics Help Runners

- Multiple studies show 1ΓÇô4% improvements in endurance time-trial performance and up to ~8% improvements in running economy after 6ΓÇô12 weeks of concurrent plyometric and running training.
[^3][^6][^9][^2][^5]
- Improvements occur without meaningful changes in VOΓéémax in many cohorts, suggesting the primary mechanism is better mechanical efficiency and SSC utilization.
[^10][^6][^11][^9]
- Moderate plyometric volumes (e.g., 30ΓÇô120 foot contacts per session) with high movement quality can be as effective as very high volumes for endurance performance.
[^4][^2][^3]

## Evidence Summary (for Agent Reasoning)

### Key Study Findings

- 6-week plyometric programs in trained distance runners improved 3-km time by ~2.7% and RE at multiple velocities without changing VOΓéémax.
[^9][^10]
- High-intensity, moderate-volume plyometric training (e.g., drop jumps plus endurance running) improved 2.4-km run times (~3.9%), 20 m sprint times (~2.3%), and jump metrics in competitive middle- and long-distance runners.
[^5]
- Intermittent sprint training and plyometric training both improved 10-km performance despite reduced weekly training mileage, supporting plyometrics as a mileage-efficient performance tool.
[^7]
- Meta-analyses and recent reviews report time-trial improvements around 1.2ΓÇô11.6%, running economy gains of 1.2ΓÇô8.1%, and sprint-performance improvements of ~1ΓÇô3.7% in endurance runners with systematic plyometric exposure.
[^6][^2][^3]
- In recreational, middle-aged runners, plyometric and resistance training similarly improved running economy, but neither consistently improved 5-km time within short interventions, highlighting dose, age, and baseline fitness effects.
[^11][^6]

### Practical Implications

- Agents should treat plyometrics as an add-on modality primarily for improving running economy, sprint ability, and robustness, not as a standalone replacement for aerobic work.
[^2][^3][^5]
- Benefits are typically observed after 6ΓÇô12 weeks of progressive training with 2ΓÇô3 plyometric exposures per week alongside regular endurance training.
[^3][^6][^2]
- Short protocols (Γëñ6 weeks, Γëñ1 session/week) can still improve performance in trained runners but may produce smaller or more variable effects in recreational runners.
[^12][^11][^9][^5]

## Athlete Profiling Logic

Agents using this skill should reason about the athlete profile before prescribing plyometrics.

### Input Dimensions

- Training age: novice (Γëñ1 year consistent running), intermediate, advanced.
[^2][^3]
- Current weekly running volume: low (<20 km), moderate (20ΓÇô50 km), high (>50 km).
[^3]
- Injury history: prior lower limb issues (Achilles, plantar fascia, knee, hip).
[^4][^2]
- Strength base: does the athlete perform regular strength training and can they squat, lunge, and hop pain-free?
[^2][^3]
- Age group: youth (<18), adult (18ΓÇô40), middle-aged (40ΓÇô60), older (>60), as responsiveness and risk profile may differ.
[^13][^6][^11]

### Readiness Checks (Preconditions)

Before high-intensity plyometrics (e.g., drop jumps, maximal bounds), agents should confirm:

- Pain-free single-leg stance for ΓëÑ20ΓÇô30 seconds per leg.
[^4][^2]
- Ability to perform basic strength moves (e.g., bodyweight squat, split squat, calf raise) without pain or major technique breakdown.
[^3][^2]
- No acute tendinopathy or bone stress injury in the lower limb.
[^4][^2]
- Ability to run continuously for at least 20ΓÇô30 minutes at easy effort without pain (for runners) or perform equivalent low-impact cardio (for cross-training athletes).
[^1][^2]

If criteria are not met, agents should:

- Prioritize a strength and movement competency phase (4ΓÇô8 weeks) before introducing higher-intensity plyometrics.
[^2][^3]
- Start with low-intensity drills (e.g., pogos, skipping) and low volumes to build tendon and musculoskeletal tolerance.
[^4][^2]

## Core Training Principles

### Frequency and Recovery

- Typical recommendation: 2 plyometric sessions per week for runners, with 48ΓÇô72 hours recovery between sessions.
[^6][^2]
- Experienced runners and in-season athletes may also benefit from 2ΓÇô4 short exposures per week (including micro-doses embedded in warm-ups), provided total volume and fatigue are managed.
[^3][^4]
- Sessions should be placed when the athlete is relatively fresh: early in a workout after dynamic warm-up or on non-fatigued days.
[^2][^4]

### Volume (Foot Contacts) and Sets/Reps

- Beginner range: ~40ΓÇô80 total foot contacts per session (e.g., 3 exercises ├ù 2ΓÇô3 sets ├ù 8ΓÇô12 reps).
[^4][^2]
- Intermediate range: ~80ΓÇô120 contacts per session with more demanding drills or additional sets.
[^2][^4]
- Advanced range: up to ~120ΓÇô300 contacts per session (highly trained athletes only), but evidence suggests moderate volumes are sufficient for endurance performance.
[^3][^2]
- Typical structure: 3ΓÇô4 exercises, 2ΓÇô6 sets each, 5ΓÇô20 repetitions depending on intensity.
[^2]

### Intensity and Progression

Agents should progress through four main levers:

- Jump height: squat jumps ΓåÆ tuck jumps ΓåÆ higher box/drop jumps.
[^1][^4]
- Jump distance: short bounds ΓåÆ long continuous bounds.
[^4][^2]
- Contact time: slower landings ΓåÆ fast, stiff contacts (e.g., pogos, hurdle hops).
[^3][^4]
- Complexity: double-leg ΓåÆ single-leg, in-place ΓåÆ continuous directional hops, adding reactive cues.
[^3][^4]

Progression rules:

- Increase total contacts per session by no more than ~10ΓÇô20% per week.
[^4][^2]
- Step back if pain, excessive soreness, or technical breakdown occurs (reduce volume, intensity, or exercise complexity).
[^2][^4]

### Placement Within the Training Week

Recommended placement logic for agents:

- Pair plyometrics with speed or interval days (before the main running workout) when quality is highest.
[^3][^4]
- Avoid heavy plyometrics immediately after long runs or heavy lifting sessions.
[^4][^2]
- For busy athletes, 10ΓÇô15 minutes of high-quality plyometrics before one tempo/interval run and one strength session per week is sufficient to maintain adaptations.
[^2][^3][^4]

## Exercise Taxonomy

Agents should select exercises that resemble running demands and match the athleteΓÇÖs level.

### Low-Intensity / Foundational Drills

- Double-leg pogos: small, springy ankle hops in place.
[^3][^4]
- Assisted pogos: using support or reduced amplitude for control.
[^4]
- Basic skipping variations: forward, lateral, and high-knee skips.
[^3][^4]
- Squat jumps and split squat jumps with controlled landings.
[^14][^4]
- Multi-directional mini hops (front/back/side) with short ranges.
[^2][^4]

Use cases:

- Early progression for novice or returning-from-injury runners.
[^2][^3]
- Warm-up and micro-dosing on easy days.
[^4][^3]

### Moderate-Intensity Drills (Running-Specific)

- Single-leg pogos in place.
[^3][^4]
- Alternating single-leg hops (e.g., hopΓÇôhopΓÇôstick patterns).
[^4][^2]
- Straight-ahead bounding (double- and single-leg bounds).
[^2][^3][^4]
- Skipping for height or distance.
[^3][^4]
- Low-height box jumps and step-off rebounds.
[^1][^4]

Use cases:

- Intermediate runners with established strength and control.
[^2][^3]
- Integration into speed workouts and stride sessions.
[^5][^4]

### High-Intensity / Advanced Drills

- Drop jumps from moderate heights (e.g., 20ΓÇô40 cm) focusing on minimal contact time and powerful rebound.
[^8][^5]
- Continuous single-leg hops with long bounds or hurdle hops.
[^4][^3]
- Reactive multidirectional hops and bounds responding to external cues.
[^4]
- Depth jumps and high box jumps for power (highly advanced only, not necessary for most endurance runners).
[^8][^5]

Use cases:

- Highly trained middle- and long-distance runners seeking marginal gains in sprint finish or mechanical efficiency.
[^12][^5]
- Short performance blocks (6ΓÇô8 weeks) aligned with key race preparation phases.
[^12][^6][^5]

## Example Protocols (Agent-Usable Templates)

### Beginner Runner Plyometric Block (6ΓÇô8 Weeks)

Context: Recreational runner, weekly volume 20ΓÇô40 km, no major injury history, limited strength background.
Goal: Improve running economy and robustness with minimal time investment.
[^6][^2]

Session structure (2├ù per week):

- Warm-up: 5ΓÇô10 minutes easy jog or cycling, dynamic leg swings/lunges.
[^1][^2]
- Main plyometric block (~10ΓÇô15 minutes):
  - Exercise 1: Double-leg pogos, 3├ù10ΓÇô15 reps.
  - Exercise 2: Squat jumps, 3├ù8ΓÇô10 reps.
  - Exercise 3: Skipping forward, 3├ù20ΓÇô30 m.
[^3][^2][^4]
- Total contacts: ~60ΓÇô90 per session.
[^2][^4]
- Progression: Add 1 set per exercise or introduce single-leg pogos in weeks 3ΓÇô4 if movement quality and soreness are acceptable.
[^4][^2]

Agents should:

- Maintain 48ΓÇô72 hours between sessions.
[^6][^2]
- Monitor soreness and joint pain; if excessive, reduce volume or regress exercises.
[^2][^4]

### Intermediate Runner Plyometric + Interval Day

Context: Intermediate runner with regular strength training, weekly volume 40ΓÇô60 km.
Goal: Enhance RE and sprint finish while supporting speed work.
[^5][^3]

Once per week (pre-intervals):

- Warm-up: 10 minutes easy jog, drills (e.g., A-skips, leg swings).
[^1]
- Plyometric block (~10 minutes):
  - Exercise 1: Single-leg pogos in place, 3├ù8ΓÇô12 reps per leg.
  - Exercise 2: Bounds (double-leg or alternating), 3├ù20ΓÇô30 m.
  - Exercise 3: Low drop jumps (20 cm), 3├ù6ΓÇô8 reps.
[^5][^4][^2]
- Total contacts: ~80ΓÇô110 per session.
[^4][^2]
- Followed by: Interval run (e.g., 6├ù800 m at 5-km pace) with adequate rest.
[^12]

Agents should:

- Avoid additional heavy plyometrics within 48 hours.
[^6][^2]
- Periodize plyometric intensity to peak 4ΓÇô6 weeks before key races.
[^12][^5]

### Advanced Block for Competitive Distance Runners

Context: Competitive middle- or long-distance runner with strong strength base and history of structured training.
Goal: Maximize RE and sprint capacity with high-intensity but moderate-volume plyometrics.
[^5][^12]

6-week block (1ΓÇô2 sessions/week):

- Warm-up: 10ΓÇô15 minutes run, dynamic drills.
[^1][^2]
- Plyometric block (~15ΓÇô20 minutes):
  - Exercise 1: Drop jumps from 20ΓÇô40 cm, 4├ù6ΓÇô8 reps.
  - Exercise 2: Continuous single-leg hurdle hops or bounds, 3├ù10ΓÇô12 contacts per leg.
  - Exercise 3: Countermovement jumps or squat jumps with maximal height, 3├ù6ΓÇô8 reps.
[^8][^5]
- Total contacts: ~80ΓÇô120 per session.
[^2][^4]

Agents should:

- Integrate block within an 8-week polarized or pyramidal training intensity distribution; evidence suggests adding plyometrics once per week can enhance 5-km performance beyond running alone.
[^12]
- Pull back or simplify drills if any signs of tendon pain or excessive fatigue occur.
[^4][^2]

## Safety, Constraints, and Anti-Patterns

### Load Management and Injury Risk

- Excessive jump volume or rapid progression increases risk of tendinopathy and joint stress; adhere to gradual volume increases and prioritize technique over intensity.
[^8][^2][^4]
- Poor landing mechanics (e.g., knees collapsing inward, repeated heavy landings, loss of posture) require immediate regression to simpler drills.
[^8][^4]
- Plyometrics should not be layered on top of already excessive running mileage without load reduction elsewhere; consider replacing some easy mileage with plyometric work to maintain total training load.
[^7][^3]

### Population-Specific Constraints

- Middle-aged or less-trained recreational runners may show improvements in running economy but not necessarily in 5-km race time in short programs; agents should set expectations accordingly and encourage longer-term progression.
[^11][^6]
- Youth athletes (9ΓÇô12 years) can benefit in sprint velocity and step length with appropriately scaled plyometrics, but require strict supervision and conservative volumes.
[^13]
- For athletes with current lower-limb pain, agents should avoid high-impact drills and instead use controlled strength work and very low-intensity plyometric variations once symptoms improve.
[^2][^4]

### Common Mistakes for Agents to Avoid

- Prescribing high-intensity plyometrics (e.g., depth jumps) to novices or injured athletes.
[^8][^4][^2]
- Ignoring recovery windows and placing plyometrics after exhaustive long runs or maximal strength sessions.
[^4][^2]
- Chasing high contact counts instead of movement quality; evidence supports moderate volumes with high-quality execution.
[^3][^2][^4]

## Summary for Agents

- Plyometric training is an evidence-backed method to improve running economy and performance, mainly through mechanical efficiency rather than large VOΓéémax changes.
[^10][^9][^6][^2]
- Effective programs typically run 6ΓÇô12 weeks, 2ΓÇô3 sessions per week, with moderate jump volumes and high movement quality.
[^6][^3][^2]
- Exercise selection should prioritize running-like drills (pogos, bounds, skipping, single-leg hops) and progress from low to high intensity based on readiness.
[^3][^4][^2]
- Safety, progression pace, and proper placement in the training week are critical to maximize benefits and minimize injury risk.
[^5][^4][^2]
- Agents should use this spec to generate structured, time-efficient, and individualized plyometric plans for runners, ensuring interoperability across different AI coding environments.
