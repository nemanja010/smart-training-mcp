# Running Zones and Testing

## Zone models overview

Running zones can be defined by pace, heart rate, or power (Stryd). The three systems are equivalent when properly anchored to the same physiological markers — but they measure different things. Power is most responsive (no cardiac drift), HR is most accessible, pace is most race-practical.

---

## Daniels 5-zone model (most widely used)

Based on VDOT (aerobic profile) and percentage of VO2max / vVO2max. Paces are per Daniels' *Running Formula*.

| Zone | Name | % VO2max | % vVO2max | Purpose |
|------|------|----------|-----------|---------|
| E | Easy | 59–74% | <76% | Aerobic base, recovery, daily miles |
| M | Marathon | 75–84% | 77–87% | Aerobic development, marathon specificity |
| T | Threshold | 83–88% | 88–92% | Lactate threshold improvement |
| I | Interval | 97–100% | 97–100% | VO2max development |
| R | Repetition | >100% | >105% | Speed, running economy, neuromuscular |

**How to find your VDOT:** Enter a recent race time (5K–marathon) into the VDOT table below or use the formula. The VDOT determines all training paces.

## Heart rate zones (Friel 7-zone model)

Anchored to Lactate Threshold Heart Rate (LTHR). To find LTHR: perform a 30-minute all-out time trial; average HR of the last 20 minutes = LTHR.

| Zone | % LTHR | Description |
|------|--------|-------------|
| Z1 | <81% | Active recovery |
| Z2 | 81–89% | Aerobic (easy) |
| Z3 | 90–93% | Tempo |
| Z4 | 94–99% | Sub-threshold |
| Z5a | 100–102% | Threshold |
| Z5b | 103–106% | Supra-threshold |
| Z5c | >106% | Anaerobic |

**Practical 3-zone model** (used in polarized training research by Seiler):

| Zone | % LTHR | HR range | Feel |
|------|--------|----------|------|
| Z1 Low | <82% | Easy | Could talk in full sentences |
| Z2 Medium | 82–88% | Moderate | Short sentences; "gray zone" |
| Z3 High | >88% | Hard | One to two words |

The polarized model says: stay in Z1 or Z3. Avoid Z2 (too hard to recover from, not hard enough to drive adaptation). This is the core of the 80/20 rule.

## Stryd running power zones (Coggan-based)

Critical Power (CP) replaces FTP. Find CP with a 20-minute all-out run (take 95% of average power) or use Stryd's auto-CP.

| Zone | % CP | Name | Purpose |
|------|------|------|---------|
| Z1 | <55% | Active recovery | Easy recovery runs |
| Z2 | 56–75% | Endurance | Aerobic base, long runs |
| Z3 | 76–87% | Tempo | Aerobic/LT development |
| Z4 | 88–95% | Threshold | LT improvement |
| Z5 | 96–105% | VO2max | VO2max |
| Z6 | 106–120% | Anaerobic | Anaerobic capacity |
| Z7 | >120% | Neuromuscular | Speed, strides |

**Stryd advantages over pace:** accounts for gradient, wind, and fatigue in real time. If using Stryd with Intervals.icu, set FTP to Critical Power and Intervals will calculate TSS automatically.

**Form Power** (Stryd metric): power generated just to keep the body upright (not forward propulsion). Should be <8% of total power. Higher values = poor running form/economy.

**Leg Spring Stiffness** (Stryd): spring-like stiffness of the leg. Higher = more elastic energy return = better economy. Increases with fitness.

## Testing protocols

### Finding LTHR (Friel 30-minute test)
1. Full warmup 15 minutes
2. 30-minute all-out time trial on flat course
3. Average HR of final 20 minutes = LTHR
4. Note: accurate only if truly all-out; should feel like a race

### Finding Critical Power (Stryd 20-minute test)
1. Full warmup 15 minutes
2. 20-minute all-out effort on flat course with Stryd
3. Average power × 0.95 = Critical Power
4. Repeat every 6–10 weeks; CP improves with training

### Finding VO2max (field test)
- **Cooper test**: distance covered in 12 minutes → VO2max = (dist_m − 504.9) / 44.73
- **1.5 mile time trial**: VO2max ≈ 3.5 + (483 / time_min)
- **Garmin / Polar estimates** from resting HR + training data (less accurate but trending is useful)

### How often to test
- Race-based VDOT: update after every goal race or time trial (every 6–12 weeks)
- LTHR: every 8–12 weeks, or when training load changes significantly
- CP: every 6–10 weeks

## Cadence

- Optimal cadence varies by runner. A universal "180 spm" target is not evidence-based.
- Increasing cadence by 5–10% can reduce ground contact time and injury risk.
- A reasonable target is your current cadence + 5% for easy runs.
- At easy pace: 160–175 spm typical for recreational runners; 175–185 for elite.

## Vertical oscillation

- Stryd and GPS watches report this as centimeters of vertical bounce per stride.
- Lower is more efficient (typical range: 7–11 cm; elite: 6–9 cm).
- Reducing vertical oscillation correlates with better running economy.
