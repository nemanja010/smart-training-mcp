# Running Injuries — Prevention and Management

**Important:** This skill can help identify risk factors, reduce injury likelihood through load management, and provide general information. It cannot diagnose injuries. Always consult a physiotherapist or sports medicine physician for persistent or acute pain.

---

## Injury risk framework

### The most common cause of running injuries

85% of running injuries are **overuse injuries** — cumulative microtrauma from training load exceeding tissue capacity.

**Risk factor hierarchy:**
1. Too much too soon (volume spike, intensity spike)
2. Too little rest (consecutive hard days)
3. Biomechanical factors (overpronation, leg length discrepancy, hip weakness)
4. Footwear issues (worn shoes, wrong type, sudden change)
5. Surface change (treadmill → road → trails)
6. Pre-existing weakness (hip abductors, glutes, calf, hip flexors)

### ACWR as injury predictor
Research (Gabbett 2016; Malone 2017 in football/soccer but applicable to running):
- ACWR 0.8–1.3: lowest injury risk
- ACWR > 1.5: injury risk doubles
- Rapid spike more dangerous than sustained high load

### CTL and ramp rate
- Ramp rate > 8–10 TSS/week in recreational runners: elevated risk
- The 10% weekly mileage increase rule is a ceiling, not a target

## Common running injuries — diagnosis and management

### Plantar fasciitis

**Location:** Heel pain, bottom of foot. Worst in first steps in the morning.
**Cause:** Inflammation of the plantar fascia (thick band connecting heel to toes). Overload from sudden mileage increase or weak intrinsic foot muscles.
**Risk factors:** High arches or flat feet, tight calves, worn shoes, sudden barefoot/minimalist transition.

**Management:**
- Ice massage (frozen water bottle rolling) 2–3 × per day
- Calf stretching (straight and bent knee — both gastrocnemius and soleus)
- Eccentric heel drops (Alfredson protocol)
- Intrinsic foot strength: towel scrunches, single-leg calf raises
- Reduce intensity; can often continue easy running
- Avoid: jumping, sudden barefoot running
- Recovery time: weeks to months; never ignore early signs

**Strength exercises:**
- Eccentric calf raise on a step: 3 × 15, twice daily
- Single-leg balance with eyes closed
- Towel scrunching with toes

---

### Achilles tendinopathy

**Location:** Pain in the Achilles tendon (back of ankle), 2–6 cm above heel.
**Types:** Mid-portion (most common) or insertional (at heel bone attachment).
**Cause:** Compressive + tensile load overload. Common after mileage increase or running on hilly terrain.

**Red flags:** Swelling, warmth, pain at rest, partial/complete rupture (felt like being kicked in the back of the leg; pop sound).

**Management (evidence-based):**
- **Eccentric loading is the gold standard** (Alfredson protocol):
  - Standing on a step, rise on two feet, lower slowly on one foot
  - 3 × 15, twice daily, through mild pain
  - 12-week commitment for results
- Heavy slow resistance (HSR) protocol (alternative): calf raises with added weight, 3 × 15 slow (3-sec up, 3-sec down), 3 × per week
- Continue running if pain ≤ 4/10 and doesn't worsen next day
- Avoid: stretching the Achilles acutely, downhill running, barefoot running
- NOT insertional: standard eccentric drops aggravate insertional; use flat-ground calf raises instead

---

### Shin splints (medial tibial stress syndrome, MTSS)

**Location:** Diffuse ache along the inner lower 2/3 of tibia (shinbone). Worsens with running, improves with rest.
**Distinguish from stress fracture:** Stress fracture = pinpoint tenderness at one spot; hop test causes sharp pain. MTSS = diffuse tenderness; hop test tolerable.

**Cause:** Overloading periosteum (bone membrane) of tibia. Too much too soon, hard surfaces, overpronation.

**Management:**
- Reduce mileage 40–60%; switch to soft surfaces (grass, trails)
- Hip strengthening (weak hip abductors = compensatory overload on tibia)
- Calf strength (eccentric calf raises)
- Check shoe wear; consider motion-control shoes if overpronating
- Ice post-run
- If not improving in 2–3 weeks: rule out stress fracture (bone scan or MRI — X-ray often misses early)

**Return to running:** Pain-free walking → pain-free easy running. Never run through >4/10 shin pain.

---

### Tibial stress fracture

**Location:** Point-tender spot on tibia. Pain during and after running.
**High-risk bones:** Tibia, navicular, femoral neck, sacrum — can progress to complete fracture.

**Action:** Stop running immediately. See physician. MRI is gold standard. Rest 6–12 weeks, then gradual return.

**Risk factors:** Female athlete triad (low energy availability + low bone density + menstrual disruption), Vitamin D/calcium deficiency, sudden high-impact surface.

---

### IT band syndrome (ITBS)

**Location:** Lateral knee pain (outside of knee). Starts after 20–40 min of running; goes away with rest.
**Cause:** Friction or compression of iliotibial band over lateral femoral condyle. Linked to weak hip abductors and external rotators.

**Management:**
- Hip strengthening: clamshells, monster walks, single-leg deadlift
- Foam rolling: IT band (mild relief), glutes and TFL (more effective source)
- Reduce downhill running
- Gait cues: slightly increase cadence, reduce crossover stride
- Anti-inflammatory phase: ice, NSAID short-term
- Recovery: weeks to months; don't force through it

**Key exercises:**
- Clamshells: 3 × 15 each side
- Side-lying hip abduction: 3 × 15 each side
- Single-leg squat (quality > quantity): 3 × 8

---

### Patellofemoral pain syndrome (runner's knee)

**Location:** Pain around or behind kneecap; worsens with hills, stairs, prolonged sitting.
**Cause:** Abnormal patellar tracking due to weak quads (VMO), weak hip abductors, or overpronation.

**Management:**
- Quad strengthening (terminal knee extension, step-downs)
- Hip abductor and external rotator strengthening
- Taping (McConnell taping can provide immediate relief)
- Avoid deep squats and stairs when acute
- Running gait: slightly forward lean, higher cadence

---

### Hip flexor / iliopsoas pain

**Location:** Front of hip/groin, deep ache or tightness.
**Cause:** Overuse, often combined with prolonged sitting (shortened hip flexors) and high weekly mileage.

**Management:**
- Hip flexor stretching (kneeling lunge stretch, not aggressive)
- Glute strengthening (reduces hip flexor dominance)
- Core work (weak core = compensatory hip flexor overuse)
- Avoid intervals that require high knee drive when symptomatic

---

### Hamstring tendinopathy

**Location:** Deep buttock or upper hamstring pain. Worsens with speed work and hills.
**Cause:** High load through the proximal hamstring tendon at 90°+ hip flexion.

**Management (evidence-based):**
- Nordic curls (gold standard for hamstring tendon loading)
- Avoid deep forward bending and stretching when acute
- Running: reduce speed work and hills; easy running often tolerable
- Return to intervals: 8–12 weeks minimum if significant
- Evidence: isometric loading in acute phase, heavy eccentric loading in subacute phase

---

### Calf strain / gastrocnemius tear

**Mechanism:** Sudden "pop" or pull sensation in calf during push-off; often in speed work.
**Grade 1 (mild):** Walk with slight limp. Grade 2 (moderate): Can't run. Grade 3 (severe): Can't bear weight.

**Management:**
- PEACE & LOVE protocol (replaces RICE):
  - **P**rotect (off it for 1–3 days)
  - **E**levate
  - **A**void anti-inflammatories in first 72 hr (impair healing)
  - **C**ompress
  - **E**ducate
  - **L**oad (start gentle loading at 72 hr)
  - **O**ptimism (healing is probable)
  - **V**ascularization (light bike, swimming)
  - **E**xercise (progressive strengthening)
- Grade 1: return 1–2 weeks. Grade 2: 3–6 weeks. Grade 3: months.

## Injury prevention — strength training prescription

The most evidence-backed injury prevention strategy for runners is progressive strength training.

### Minimum effective dose
- 2 × per week in base/build phase
- 1 × per week in peak phase

### Priority exercises for runners

**Hip complex (most important for injury prevention):**
- Single-leg squat / pistol squat progression
- Romanian deadlift (single leg)
- Clamshells with resistance band
- Hip thrust / glute bridge
- Monster walk (resistance band lateral steps)

**Calf and Achilles:**
- Single-leg calf raise (full range on step)
- Eccentric single-leg heel drop
- Jump rope (builds tendon resilience)

**Core:**
- Copenhagen plank (adductor + core)
- Dead bug
- Pallof press
- Single-leg balance with perturbation

**Foot intrinsic:**
- Towel scrunches
- Short foot exercise (arch doming)
- Single-leg calf raise on unstable surface

### Plyometric progression (advanced, for experienced runners)
- Only after 8–12 weeks of heavy slow resistance base
- Adds tendon elasticity and running economy
- Box jumps, bounding, single-leg hops
- 1 session/week in base/build; stop 3 weeks before key race

## The 10-minute rule for pain

| Pain level (0–10) | During run | Action |
|------------------|-----------|--------|
| 0 | None | Normal training |
| 1–3 | Mild, goes away quickly | Monitor; note if worsens |
| 3–4 | Moderate, settles during run | Reduce intensity/duration; see physio if persistent |
| 5+ | Significant | Stop; rest; seek assessment |
| Any pain that worsens through the run | — | Stop immediately |

**The 24-hour rule:** If pain >4/10 the morning after a run, the run was too much. Reduce load.

---

## Coming back after injury

1. Pain-free walking for 1 week
2. Run/walk intervals: 1 min run, 2 min walk × 20 min — pain-free
3. Progress run/walk ratio over 2 weeks
4. Continuous easy 20 min run — pain-free
5. Rebuild at ≤50% of pre-injury volume
6. No quality sessions for first 4 weeks back

Don't try to make up lost fitness. The fastest return is the slowest buildup.
